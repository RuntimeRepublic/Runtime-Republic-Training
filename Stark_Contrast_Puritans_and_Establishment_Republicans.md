# Stark Contrast: Puritans and Establishment Republicans

*(Approximately 7 minutes when read aloud)*

One group treated a fixed standard as something elites could not quietly rewrite. The other has repeatedly treated institutional continuity, donor consensus, and procedural norms as the effective standard—even when those norms diverge from the written rules they claim to defend. The difference is the same one that separates ordered agency from elite capture.

The Puritan measure, as stated in the Agentic Puritan Revival thesis, is clear. Liberty in the open state is measured by the degree to which ideas, practices, and institutions escape elite capture. Elite capture occurs when human authorities add, subtract, or invent binding requirements beyond what the standard itself prescribes. The Regulative Principle supplies the model: the acceptable way is instituted by the standard and limited by it. What is commanded is permitted. What is not commanded is forbidden in the regulated sphere. Silence is restrictive, not an invitation for human invention. Magistrates and innovators possess no residual authority to bind conscience once the standard has spoken or remained silent.

Under the Clarendon Code, Puritans and Nonconformists lost official places. The established institutions had been captured. They responded by building parallel structures of self-examination, mutual accountability, practical education, and commercial discipline. Agency increased as dependence on elite-defined rules decreased. In New England the same habit produced covenants and written limits that treated authority as derived rather than original. These were not perfect societies. Their distinctive success was the refusal to let the imaginations of men become the binding rule.

Establishment Republicans—those who opposed Trump and continued to prioritize institutional gatekeeping—have often operated by a different measure. The written standard (the Constitution, platform planks, or statutory limits) is acknowledged, yet the effective standard frequently becomes the preservation of existing institutions, donor networks, procedural consensus, or “norms” that are not themselves written into the governing documents. When voter majorities or primary outcomes diverge from that consensus, the response has frequently been to treat the divergence itself as illegitimate rather than to measure it against the fixed standard. Silence in the Constitution or platform is treated as permissive of elite devices rather than restrictive of them.

The practical outcomes diverge under pressure. When official institutions were closed to them, Puritans continued to produce, trade, educate, and discipline themselves. Their capacity did not depend on state permission. Establishment Republican resistance to Trump-era outcomes has more often taken the form of institutional refusal—committee control, donor redirection, media coordination, or procedural obstruction—than the construction of parallel capacity under a non-capturable standard. Authority is treated as residing in the continuity of the institution and its recognized gatekeepers rather than as derived from a standard those gatekeepers cannot rewrite.

This is not a claim that every critic of Trump is identical, nor that every establishment figure lacks principle. It is a claim about the dominant pattern: one side treats the standard as non-capturable and builds capacity when the institutions are closed; the other treats capture of the practical standard as the ordinary means of maintaining position. The modern extension of the Puritan pattern treats control documents as the fixed standard. Lessons teach; the standard rules. Success is measured by whether participants can act, detect deviation, and correct course without waiting for elite permission or accepting unauthorized additions.

The contrast is stark because one side measures liberty by escape from elite capture of the standard, and the other has repeatedly measured success by the preservation of the institutions that perform the capture. The difference is not abstract. It is the difference between systems that build capacity under exclusion and systems that require exclusion of disruptive majorities to maintain the existing order of authority.
