# How Puritans Would React to the Kansas Tax System

*(Pattern applied from the Agentic Puritan Revival thesis)*

Puritans would measure the Kansas tax system by one test: does it escape elite capture of the standard, or does it invite human devices to become binding?

The standard is the written limit—constitutional enumeration of taxing power, consent of the governed through clear rules, and the refusal to treat silence or discretion as a license to invent new burdens. Under the Regulative Principle analog, what is authorized is permitted; what is not authorized is forbidden in the regulated sphere. Silence is restrictive. Magistrates, boards, and innovators possess no residual authority to bind once the standard has spoken or remained silent. Elite capture occurs when appraisers, school boards, county commissions, or legislative majorities add requirements, valuations, or spending mandates beyond what the fixed rule prescribes.

The current Kansas system fails that test on several points. Property taxes rest on ongoing discretionary appraisal and local spending choices that treat the absence of a hard cap as permission to expand. School funding formulas and local option taxes allow officials to invent binding claims on property without a fixed, non-capturable measure of what may be taken. Sinking funds, incentives, and abatements often function as elite devices—authorized in practice by the imaginations of boards rather than by a clear, prior standard. The result is dependence on those who control valuation and budgeting rather than capacity that survives their decisions.

Puritans under the Clarendon Code did not wait for captured institutions to reform themselves. They built parallel structures of self-examination, mutual accountability, practical education, and commercial discipline. Agency increased as dependence on elite-defined rules decreased. Applied to Kansas taxes, the same habit would produce specific changes:

- Replace open-ended property taxation with a fixed, phase-out schedule grounded in a broad-base consumption tax. The rule would be written in advance, not left to annual appraisal or board discretion. What is not authorized by the phase-out statute is forbidden.
- Require every local spending authority to operate under enumerated limits and public, auditable sinking funds invested only in instruments the standard itself names (for example, short-term Treasury instruments). Silence in the standard would not permit creative off-budget devices.
- Treat school and local government finance as subject to the same non-capturable measure. Formulas that invite annual renegotiation by elites would be replaced by fixed per-pupil or per-jurisdiction rules that cannot be quietly rewritten. Rural protections and school choice would be written as explicit limits, not as discretionary exceptions.
- Demand continuous public measurement—published valuations, spending against the fixed rule, and deviation reports—so that ordinary citizens can detect capture without waiting for official permission. This mirrors the Puritan diary and meeting discipline: record, compare to the standard, correct.
- Refuse rebates or targeted relief that function as new elite inventions. Relief would come only through the phase-out of the unauthorized burden itself, not through additional discretionary distributions.

The practical outcome they would seek is the same one they produced under exclusion: capacity that does not depend on the continued goodwill of captured institutions. Commercial activity, household budgeting, and local association would continue under a clear rule rather than under the annual devices of appraisers and boards. Authority would be treated as derived from the written standard, not original in the officials who administer it.

This is not a claim that every current Kansas official lacks principle, nor that the transition would be costless. It is the application of the Puritan measure: escape from elite capture of the standard, ordered agency under a fixed rule, and the construction of parallel capacity when the official system treats silence as a license to invent binding requirements. The thesis tests whether that pattern can again produce ordered liberty that does not depend on the imaginations of those who hold power. Applied to Kansas taxes, the first step is to write the limit so that no board can quietly rewrite it.
