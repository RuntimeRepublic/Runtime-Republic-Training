# Stark Contrast: The Salem Witch Trials and the Soviet Show Trials

*(Approximately 7 minutes when read aloud)*

Both were trials of hidden enemies. Both ran on accusation and confession. Both killed innocent people. On the surface they look like the same failure in two different centuries. They are not. One was a breach of a fixed standard that was later corrected by returning to that standard. The other was the ordinary working of a system in which the people who held power were the standard. The difference is the one that separates elite capture as a failure from elite capture as a method of rule.

## What they shared

In 1692, in Salem Village and the towns around it, Massachusetts searched for people secretly in league with the devil. In Moscow between 1936 and 1938, the Soviet state searched for secret Trotskyites, wreckers, and foreign agents. In both places, ordinary conduct could be read as a disguise. A neighbor's quarrel or a factory breakdown became evidence of hidden guilt.

Both relied on evidence that could not be tested. The Salem court accepted spectral evidence: a witness's claim to have seen the accused person's spirit tormenting them. No one else could see it, and the accused could not disprove it. The Moscow trials rested almost entirely on confessions, with little physical proof behind them. In both, the safest course for an accused person was often to name others, and so the circle of accusation widened.

## Where they began

Salem began from below. It started with afflicted girls in one village, spread through local grievances, and was taken up by a special court that Governor William Phips created to hear the cases. It was a panic that the authorities joined and then failed to restrain.

The Moscow trials began from above. They were planned by Joseph Stalin and staged by the prosecutor Andrei Vyshinsky. The defendants, among them Grigory Zinoviev, Lev Kamenev, and Nikolai Bukharin, were former leaders of the party. The verdicts were settled before the trials opened. The courtroom was a stage, and the audience was the nation and the world.

## What a confession meant

At Salem, about fifty people confessed to witchcraft, and none of the confessors was hanged. The nineteen who were hanged were those who would not confess, people who refused to lie about themselves even to save their lives. The court's incentive was perverse, but it still left room for a conscience that would not bend.

In Moscow, a confession was the road to execution. Confessions were extracted through sleep deprivation, beatings, and threats against wives and children. Their purpose was not to find the truth. It was to make the accused give public consent to the party's version of events. Bukharin confessed in March 1938 and was shot days later.

## The scale

At Salem, about two hundred people were accused. Nineteen were hanged, one man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death under stones, and several more died in jail. It was a grave injustice in a small colony.

The show trials were only the public face of the Great Terror. In 1937 and 1938, by the Soviet secret police's own records, roughly 680,000 people were executed, and many hundreds of thousands more were sent to the labor camps. The difference is not one of degree. It is the difference between a local breakdown and a machine.

## How each one ended

Salem was stopped from within its own system. In October 1692 the minister Increase Mather argued that spectral evidence was not enough to convict, and that it was better for ten suspected witches to escape than for one innocent person to be condemned. Governor Phips dissolved the special court. The new court that replaced it refused spectral evidence, and nearly every remaining defendant was acquitted.

The Soviet trials were never stopped by an appeal to a higher rule, because there was none. They ended when the party chose to end them. Correction waited for a change in leadership.

## Repentance and repair

In 1697 Massachusetts held a public day of fasting for the wrongs done at Salem. That day, Judge Samuel Sewall stood in his church while his confession of guilt was read aloud. Twelve jurors signed a statement admitting they had been deceived and asking forgiveness. In 1711 the colony's legislature reversed the convictions of the condemned and paid compensation to their families.

In the Soviet Union, Nikita Khrushchev's secret speech of 1956 began to admit the crimes of the Terror, but most of the Moscow defendants were not cleared until 1988, half a century after they were shot. Repair, when it came, was granted by the same party that had done the killing.

## The measure

The Agentic Puritan Revival measures liberty by the degree to which ideas, practices, and institutions escape elite capture. Elite capture occurs when human authorities add binding requirements beyond what the standard prescribes.

By that measure, Salem was a capture of the standard. Spectral evidence was a human device added to the rules of proof. The judges let their own reading become the binding rule, and innocent people died. The guard failed because the leaders stopped testing themselves against the standard. Yet the standard still stood above them. When Mather and others appealed to it, the judges had to answer to it, the court was closed, and within a few years the guilty stood up in public and confessed.

The Soviet system had no such standard. The party wrote the rule, interpreted the rule, enforced the rule, and judged anyone who questioned it. There was nothing above the accuser to appeal to. Capture was not a breach of the system; it was the system.

## The lesson

The lesson is not that the Puritans were innocent. They were not, and their failure should be told plainly. The lesson is that a fixed standard above those who rule does not prevent every injustice, but it makes correction possible. Salem shows elite capture breaking out and then being reversed by the standard it had violated. Moscow shows what happens when there is no standard left to reverse it.

The modern extension of the Puritan pattern treats control documents as that fixed standard. Lessons teach; the standard rules. Success is measured by whether participants can detect a deviation, appeal to the written rule, and correct course, even when the people who made the mistake are the people in charge.
