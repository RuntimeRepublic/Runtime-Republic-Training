Gary had the look of a man reading a ransom note.

It was a Thursday night, four days after payday, and he sat at Mike's kitchen table holding his phone close to his face. The bank app glowed back at him. He scrolled down, then up, then down again, as if the money might be hiding below the screen.

"I swear," he said, "aliens are taking it."

Mike set two cups of coffee on the table and sat down across from him. He didn't laugh. That surprised Gary a little. Mike usually laughed at everything.

"Feels that way, doesn't it?" Mike said.

"Feels that way? Mike, I got paid Monday. It's Thursday. Where did it go? I didn't buy a boat. I didn't take a trip. I bought groceries and gas and paid the light bill, and now look." He turned the phone around. "That's what's left."

Mike looked at the number and nodded slowly. "A year ago I'd have said the same thing. Word for word."

"And now?"

"Now I know where mine goes."

Gary set the phone face down on the table, the way people do when they don't want to look at something anymore. "Okay. I'll bite. How?"

Mike pulled his laptop closer and opened it, but he didn't turn it around yet. "Last fall I started running my budget with AI. Nothing fancy. I connected my accounts, dropped in my bills, and asked it one question: where does my money go?"

"And it told you?"

"It told me things I didn't want to hear." Mike smiled for the first time. "The first month, it found four subscriptions I'd forgotten I had. A streaming service I hadn't opened since the playoffs. A cloud storage plan for a phone I don't own anymore. An app I signed up for during a free trial, three years ago. And a magazine. I don't even read magazines."

Gary laughed. "How much?"

"Enough that I was mad at myself. I cancelled all four that night." Mike shrugged. "Here's the thing. I wasn't being robbed by aliens. I'd just lost track."

Gary picked up his coffee and held it without drinking. "So you're saying I'm wasting money on magazines."

"I'm saying something bigger than that." Mike leaned back. "Subscriptions are small. You know what else we all lost track of a long time ago? Taxes."

"I know what I pay in taxes. It's on my W-2."

"Some of it is. But think about how most of it reaches you. Some of it is gone before your paycheck lands. You never touch it. Some of it gets added on top of your bills, down at the bottom in tiny print. Some of it is folded into your rent, or into the price of everything on the shelf, so you never see a line for it at all." Mike tapped the table. "Most people can't tell you how many different governments take a piece of their money every month. Not because they're dumb. Because the system was never built for them to see it."

Gary was quiet for a moment. "So it's not aliens."

"It's not aliens. But it feels exactly the same." Mike grinned. "There's a name for where you are right now. We call it CE1. Civic Encounter, level one. Something took your money. You saw it happen. But you can't explain it yet."

"Is there a level two?"

"There are five." Mike finally turned the laptop around. On the screen was a simple page with a column of numbers, every one of them labeled. "But you don't start at five. You start with one challenge."

"Here it comes."

"Track your money for one month. Every dollar in, every dollar out. And while you're at it, find every tax. Not just the income tax. Every tax and every fee, on every bill, every receipt and every pay stub. Write each one down, and who gets it."

Gary looked at the laptop, then at his phone, then back at Mike. "I don't have one of those."

"A laptop?"

"I've got this phone, and a mailbox full of envelopes. My bills still come on paper. My electric company sends me a letter every month like it's 1985."

"Good," Mike said.

"Good?"

"Start with what you've got. Don't throw those envelopes away. Keep every one. Your pay stub, your utility bill, and a receipt from the grocery store. That's enough to start." He closed the laptop. "We'll meet next week, online. You can join by phone."

Gary sighed the sigh of a man who already knew he'd say yes. "Fine. One month. Every tax."

"Every tax."

They shook on it across the table, the way people used to seal a deal before contracts got sixty pages long.

Gary stood up and pulled on his jacket. At the door he stopped. "What do I need to bring next week?"

"Three documents," Mike said. "The pay stub, the utility bill and the receipt." He paused. "And bring stamps."

Gary frowned. "Stamps? Why would I need stamps? You just told me I'm going digital."

Mike smiled and picked up the coffee cups.

"You'll see."

## Recap

- Most of us lost track of our taxes the same way we lose track of subscriptions.
- The challenge: track one month and find every tax, on every bill, receipt and pay stub.
- CE1 is where everyone starts: you saw it, but you can't explain it yet.
