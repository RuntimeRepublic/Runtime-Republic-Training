# The Landlord and His Bookkeeper

Nobody expected Linda's landlord to show up.

She had asked him on a Saturday, standing on the porch while he fixed a loose gutter. She asked politely, the way Mike had said to: how much of her rent went to property tax? Then she added the line. "We're working on ideas to end property taxes."

He had stopped working on the gutter and come down the ladder.

Now, on Thursday night, there were five faces on the call instead of three. Mike on his laptop. Gary on his phone, propped against the coffee maker again. Linda at her kitchen table. And two new squares: a man in his sixties with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, and a woman with a spreadsheet already open on a second monitor behind her.

"This is Walt," Linda said. "He owns my house. And three others on the street."

"And this is Peggy," Walt said, nodding at the other square. "She keeps my books. Has for nineteen years. If anybody's going to talk numbers, it's her, not me."

Mike welcomed them both. Walt didn't smile back.

"Let me say something up front," Walt said, "because I've been to meetings like this before. Somebody always ends up pointing at the landlord. Rent's too high, the landlord's greedy, the landlord's the problem." He folded his arms. "So before anybody starts: I didn't raise Linda's rent to buy a boat."

"Nobody here thinks you did," Mike said.

"Good. Because here's what rent pays for. The mortgage on that house. Insurance, which doubled in three years. Repairs. That gutter. A water heater last winter. And the property tax bill, which goes up every year whether I like it or not, and I don't." He sat back. "I don't set that bill. I just pay it."

"Then you're exactly who we need," Mike said. "Peggy, can you show us how it works?"

Peggy shared her screen. It was a simple sheet, no names, no addresses, just a few rows. "I made this for tonight," she said. "Linda's house, as an example, but I've taken out anything personal. These are rough numbers, so call it an estimate."

She highlighted one row.

"Out of a year of rent on that house, about one month's rent goes to the property tax bill." She let that sit. "One month in twelve. Before insurance, before repairs, before Walt sees a dime of profit."

Linda stared at the screen. "One whole month?"

"Roughly. It's an estimate. But it's close."

"So one month a year," Linda said slowly, "I'm basically renting from the county."

Walt laughed in spite of himself. "That's about right."

Something changed on the call then. Gary felt it even through his phone speaker. Linda had come in thinking Walt was the one taking her money. Walt had come in expecting to be blamed. Now they were both looking at the same row on Peggy's screen.

"He's not taking it," Linda said quietly. "He's passing it through."

"And I'm the one who gets yelled at when it goes up," Walt said. "So are you, a little. Everybody blames 'high rent.' Nobody blames the tax bill inside it."

"Same side," Mike said.

Walt looked at Linda's square for a long moment. "Huh. Same side."

"There's one more thing," Peggy said, scrolling down. "It's not just the tax. It's the time. Walt's a small business. Every year I spend hours on his tax paperwork: the rental income forms, the depreciation schedules, the county assessments, the appeals when the valuation jumps. Hours he pays me for. Hours that build nothing."

Gary frowned. "So the tax costs money, and then figuring out the tax costs more money?"

"Now you're getting it," Peggy said. "In my old job we'd call that waste. Pure waste."

Walt uncrossed his arms. "So what is this, exactly? A club?"

"A team," Mike said. "Some people call theirs a study circle. We track every tax, find who gets it and who voted for it, and we bring evidence instead of anger."

"Could landlords have their own team?" Walt asked. "I know a dozen owners who'd sit in on something like this."

"Absolutely. With one rule," Mike said. "Everyone on a business team also belongs to a consumer team, like this one. You pay taxes as a household too. That way nobody ends up on opposite sides of the table."

Walt thought about it. "So I stay here."

"You stay here."

"Fine by me." He finally smiled. "Linda's the best tenant I've got. I'd rather be on her side than across from her."

Linda smiled back. "You fixed the gutter. You're on probation."

Everyone laughed, even Peggy.

When the call wrapped up, Gary counted squares. Five people: a renter, an owner, a bookkeeper, and two friends who had started with a joke about aliens.

"Next week," Peggy said before she logged off, "let's do something different. Everybody bring a list of where your money goes, in the order it actually leaves. Not the order you'd like. The real order."

"Who gets paid first," Mike said.

"Exactly," said Peggy. "Let's find out."

## Recap

- A tax can pass through several hands before anyone sees it: Linda's rent carries Walt's property tax bill.
- An estimate is evidence when it's clearly labeled as an estimate.
- Business owners are welcome, and everyone also stays on a consumer team, so no one is on opposite sides.
