The first online meeting started four minutes late, because Gary couldn't find the mute button.

"Can you hear me? Mike? Is this thing on?"

"I can hear you fine," Mike said from his laptop. "Everybody in your kitchen can probably hear you too."

Gary was propped against his coffee maker, phone in one hand, a stack of envelopes fanned across the counter like a losing poker hand. "Okay. I brought the three documents. Pay stub, light bill, grocery receipt."

"And the stamps?"

"I brought the stamps. I still don't know why."

"Good. Start with the pay stub."

Gary unfolded it and squinted. "Gross pay. Then a bunch of lines. Federal income tax. Social Security. Medicare. State income tax." He stopped. "Mike, all of this comes out before I ever see the money."

"Every penny. That's called withholding. The government gets paid on payday. You get paid after."

"I always just looked at the bottom number."

"Most people do. That's the point." Mike's voice was calm, almost cheerful. "How many governments so far?"

Gary counted on his fingers. "Federal. Social Security and Medicare are federal too, right? So one. And the state. Two."

"Now the light bill."

Gary turned the envelope over. Down at the bottom, under the kilowatt hours, were lines he had never read in twenty years of paying the bill. "There's a franchise fee. And a sales tax. And something called a, hang on, an energy cost adjustment. Is that a tax?"

"Good question. Write it down with a question mark. Some lines are taxes, some are the company's charges, and the bill doesn't always tell you which. We find out."

"The franchise fee goes to the city?"

"Usually. Look it up later. Write it down."

Gary wrote. His handwriting got smaller as the list got longer. "So the city's in it too. That's three. Maybe four with the county, if the sales tax splits."

"You're doing great."

On Mike's screen, a different window was open. While Gary read line by line, Mike had dropped his own utility bill into his AI project and asked it to list every tax and fee. It took about eight seconds. Mike didn't mention that yet. He wanted Gary to feel it first: what it's like to dig every number out by hand.

Then a voice came from Gary's end that wasn't Gary's.

"Is this the tax thing?"

Gary turned. "Oh. Mike, this is Linda. She lives next door. She saw the envelopes through the window and asked what I was doing, and I said I was hunting aliens, and she said she wanted in."

Mike laughed. "Welcome, Linda. Are you an alien hunter too?"

Linda leaned into the frame. She had a toddler on her hip and the tired, practical look of someone who had paid every bill in her house for years. "I rent," she said. "Me, my mom, and two kids. And I'll tell you exactly where my money's going. My rent went up again in August. Third time in four years."

"Did the landlord say why?"

"Taxes. He said property taxes went up." She shifted the toddler to her other hip. "Which is funny, because I've never gotten a property tax bill in my life."

"You pay it, though," Mike said.

"How? I don't own the house."

"Your landlord pays the county. Where does he get the money?"

Linda opened her mouth, then closed it. "From my rent."

"From your rent. Every month, a piece of your rent is property tax. You just never see the line."

"How big a piece?"

"That's the question," Mike said. "Nobody on this call knows. I don't, and Gary sure doesn't."

"I barely know what's on my light bill," Gary said.

Linda was quiet for a moment. "So how do I find out?"

"Ask him."

"Ask my landlord? He'll think I'm about to fight the rent."

"So tell him the truth. Ask him for an estimate of how much of your rent goes to property tax. Then tell him one more thing." Mike paused. "Tell him, 'We're working on ideas to end property taxes.'"

Linda laughed out loud. "End them? Can you do that?"

"Some people think so. That's part of what this group is for. But first we need the numbers."

"And if he won't answer?"

"Then that's evidence too," Mike said. "Even no answer tells us something."

Gary was grinning at his phone. "Look at us. A real investigation."

"Write down your four governments, Gary. Linda, you've got homework. Same time next week?"

"Same time," Linda said. "Gary, add me to whatever this is."

"It's a group chat," Gary said. "I know how to do that, at least."

When the call ended, Gary looked at the envelopes on his counter. Four governments, maybe five, in just three pieces of paper. And somewhere down the street, inside a rent check, was a number nobody had ever shown him.

He put the stamps back in the drawer. He still didn't know what they were for.

## Recap

- Withholding means government gets paid before your family sees the money.
- Renters pay property tax too, hidden inside their rent.
- Asking the person who pays the bill is the first step, and even no answer is evidence.
