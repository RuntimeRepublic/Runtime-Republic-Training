# What If Only H.R. 25 Passed?

Here's a thought experiment. Suppose Congress passed one bill, H.R. 25, the FairTax Act, and nothing else changed. Where would the Civic Encounters Challenge stand?

First, what the bill does. As introduced in January 2025, H.R. 25 repeals the federal income tax, payroll taxes, and the estate and gift taxes. It replaces them with one national sales tax of twenty-three percent, starting in 2027. It abolishes the IRS. The states collect the tax and keep a small fee for doing it. And every registered household gets a monthly payment from Social Security that covers the tax on spending up to the poverty level.

The American Gold Contract for Liberty calls passing H.R. 25 the first step to ending the tax trap. So let's see how far that first step takes us.

Start at home. For a family, the change is big. The federal tax leaves your paycheck and shows up on every receipt instead. A month of receipts proves exactly what you paid. Encounter two, Physical Evidence, gets easy, and the federal part of encounter three, Contact, is nearly automatic.

But your property tax notice doesn't change at all. Neither do the fees hiding on your phone and power bills. So for state and local government, most families would still be stuck at encounter one or two.

Now the national level. Everyone sees the federal tax at the register. That's encounter one and two for the whole country. But encounter three only gets halfway. You'd know exactly what Washington takes. You still wouldn't know where it goes, because the bill doesn't trace spending.

And the local and worldwide levels? They don't move at all.

So the honest answer is this. H.R. 25 alone is a huge win for seeing what's taken. It finishes the first step and nothing beyond it. No level reaches encounter five.

Now, the critics. And the answers. Fair is fair.

Critics say a twenty-three percent sales tax will feel like sticker shock on every purchase. The answer: the shock is the point. Today's federal tax is hidden in withholding and in prices, so families can't add it up at all. A tax you can see is a tax you can judge. Adam Smith said it in 1776: "The tax which each individual is bound to pay ought to be certain, and not arbitrary."

Critics say a high sales tax invites cheating, with cash deals under the table. The answer: today's system already leaks. The IRS projected a gap of six hundred ninety-six billion dollars between what was owed and what was paid for tax year 2022. Both systems have cheaters. The fair question is which one has fewer.

Critics say asking fifty states to collect a federal tax is a burden. The answer: Americans already spend about six point nine billion hours a year complying with federal tax rules, and most states already run sales taxes.

Critics say the burden could shift toward middle-income families. That one is a fair question, and it's still open. Answering it takes a careful side-by-side comparison with today's income and payroll taxes.

And the strongest criticism of all: the bill doesn't actually repeal the Sixteenth Amendment. It only says Congress finds that it should be repealed. So a future Congress could bring back an income tax on top of the sales tax. Two taxes instead of one. Milton Friedman warned, "Nothing is so permanent as a temporary government program." Promises made by one Congress can be undone by the next. That risk is real, and the only full answer is a constitutional amendment, a rule that the next majority can't quietly change.

Which brings us to the path. You can't leave a bad system and wait for a perfect one. But you can move to an imperfect one that sends clear signals, takes the whole tax bite at one time in one place, and has a path forward.

After H.R. 25, that path runs through the next steps of the American Gold Contract for Liberty. Share revenue with states and towns so they can end property and income taxes. Tag every federal dollar as either an enumerated power or not, on a public ledger. Give every citizen a Power of 72 savings account. And adopt the other two frameworks at every level of government.

One bill can open the door. It takes the rest of the plan to walk through it.

## Recap

Let's sum up. If only H.R. 25 passed, federal taxes would leave the paycheck and show up on every receipt. Families and the nation would reach encounters one and two for federal taxes, and encounter three would be halfway done, because spending still isn't traced. Property tax and local fees wouldn't change. Critics raise sticker shock, cheating, state burden, and who pays more. Most of those problems exist today too, but who pays more is still open. Adam Smith said a tax should be certain, not arbitrary, and Milton Friedman warned that nothing is so permanent as a temporary government program. The biggest risk is an income tax returning without a Sixteenth Amendment repeal. H.R. 25 is the first step, not the last. Thanks for listening. This has been Runtime Republic.
