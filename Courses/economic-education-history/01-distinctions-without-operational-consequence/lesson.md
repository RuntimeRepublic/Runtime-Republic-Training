## The thesis

Academic distinctions between the Nazi and Soviet systems are formalistic exercises with little realized value for anyone assessing the actual exercise of power. That includes claims that the Nazis were not truly socialist, or that formal private ownership under them created a meaningful difference.

## Socialist language, rival socialists

Both movements presented themselves in socialist language and then treated rival socialists as illegitimate. The Nazis, whose full name was the National Socialist German Workers’ Party, suppressed the SPD and KPD, smashed independent trade unions, and filled the first concentration camps with those political opponents. They justified the suppression by declaring Marxist parties false, internationalist, or foreign-influenced, while reserving authentic national socialism for themselves.

The Bolsheviks likewise denounced other socialists as deviationists and subjected their own party to repeated purges. In both cases the claim that competitors “do not do it correctly” functioned as a tool to monopolize power rather than as evidence of a deep programmatic divide.

International affiliations (Comintern for the KPD, the Labour and Socialist International for the SPD) were real, yet both parties remained German organizations operating in German politics. The Nazis themselves ran a foreign network: the party's Foreign Organization enrolled members abroad from 1931, and in February 1939 the German American Bund filled Madison Square Garden with some 20,000 people. Calling rivals foreign-influenced was a weapon, not a distinction.

## Property that cannot survive state capture

On property, title which cannot survive state capture is not private property in any operative sense. Nazi policy left legal ownership of most industrial firms in private hands and preserved residual profit claims for non-Jewish owners. Yet the regime subordinated that ownership to political directives, reserved the right to intervene or seize, and systematically stripped Jewish owners.

The cases are concrete. Hugo Junkers, the aviation pioneer, was forced out of his own aircraft company between 1933 and 1935, and the state took it over. Fritz Thyssen, an industrialist who had helped fund the Nazis' rise, fled Germany in 1939, and the regime seized his holdings. If even an early backer of the party could not keep his property once he broke with it, the title never protected anything the regime wanted.

Consider a typical factory owner who arrived to find party symbols and political oversight imposed on his plant: he experienced the practical nullity of the formal right. Soviet nationalization was more explicit. Under collectivization from 1929, peasants were stripped of their land and livestock and forced into collective farms, and those branded kulaks lost everything and were deported. The shared outcome was that residual control rested with the regime, not the nominal owner. Distinctions that survive only in legal form or in academic classification do not alter that result.

Some economic historians, notably Christoph Buchheim and Jonas Scherner, answer that firms kept room to maneuver. They could sometimes decline state-requested investments they judged unprofitable, and the regime then built its own plants, such as the Hermann Göring Works, rather than compel them. That is bargaining over terms inside a captured system, not ownership. No major manufacturer stayed outside the war economy. A liberty that never produced a different result for owners or workers is not a real difference—it is academic entertainment.

## Victims and the operation of the systems

The same pattern appears in the selection of victims and the operation of the systems. Both regimes applied collective violence to defined population groups. The Nazis did it through racial categories culminating in the killing centers. The Soviets did it through class, political, and ethnic categories that included mass deportations of Chechens, Crimean Tatars, Volga Germans and others, and the Ukrainian famine.

Critics answer that the Holocaust alone aimed at the total destruction of a people. Yet in 2018 a UN-backed court convicted Khmer Rouge leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan of genocide against ethnic Vietnamese, and Nuon Chea also against the Cham. And at Nazino in 1933, Soviet authorities left some 6,000 "declassed elements" on a Siberian river island with almost no food; thousands died within weeks. A communist regime is on the record for genocidal intent, and the Soviet one destroyed people by label.

Both maintained camps that began with political prisoners and both sustained leaderships that lived far better than the populations they ruled. The Nuremberg tribunal convicted the regime's leaders for its concrete crimes, and German courts later convicted even field marshals—a Munich court sentenced Ferdinand Schörner in 1957 for ordering the summary execution of his own soldiers. Those convictions establish what was done, not that formal differences in ownership statutes or self-description produced different realities of arbitrary power.

## Distinctions without operational consequence

Academic efforts to separate the two systems by reference to residual private title, the precise wording of platforms, or the presence of international ties register as distinctions without operational consequence.

For an independent assessment of how power was actually organized and used, the shared features carry the weight: subordination of property and persons to the regime, destruction of independent worker organizations, internal purges, collective repression of population groups, and privileged elites. Formal or definitional differences that do not constrain the state’s ability to capture property, eliminate rivals, or select groups for destruction remain without realized value.
