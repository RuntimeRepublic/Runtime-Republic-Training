# The Keg Question

Course: Every Dollar in the Open
Lesson: 02 of 06
Status: Draft
Runtime: about 6 minutes
Image: Pending
Podcast: Not recorded
Sources: None
Approved by: —

Audience: Team treasurers, admins and members

---

The Sedgwick County team started the way most groups start: with an envelope.

It was a plain white envelope kept in the treasurer's truck. Members dropped in five or ten dollars at meetings. When the team needed stamps or printer paper, the treasurer paid with his own card and took cash back out of the envelope. When someone ordered pizza, the treasurer paid through his phone, and people paid him back, mostly.

It worked for about four months.

Then a member, call sign Ridgeline, asked a simple question at a Saturday meeting. "What did the sixty dollars from October go to?"

The treasurer thought about it. Flyers, probably. Maybe part of the pizza. The receipt was somewhere in the truck. His own grocery money had moved through the same phone app that week. Nobody in the room thought he had done anything wrong. But nobody in the room could prove he hadn't, including him.

That was the night the team decided to stop running on trust that nobody could check.

They didn't just open a bank account. They registered the group with the Kansas Secretary of State, and they wrote down why: to test, for real, whether a small group could run every dollar in the open. Donations stamped on the blockchain. Spending approved in public. Every member able to trace their own money without anyone learning who gave what.

Setup took longer than anyone expected: forms, a tax ID, a bank that wanted two signers, and two admins chosen from members who had signed the values. Then the first receipt codes went out, private strings of letters and numbers, one per donation. Longbow got his and put it in his notes app.

The first month's file went up on GitHub. Gifts were cut into ten-dollar blocks. No names, no call signs, just scrambled codes, amounts, and the week. The treasurer ran the file through OpenTimestamps, and its fingerprint went onto the Bitcoin blockchain.

It worked. Longbow typed his code into the lookup page on his phone and saw his three blocks sitting in the pool, waiting.

And then the first spending request came in, and the team found out the system had been the easy part.

The request was for a cookout. Forty-two dollars for burgers, buns and charcoal. An admin opened the SPEND issue. The second admin went to approve it and stopped.

"Wait. Is this something our money is for?"

Nobody had ever said.

So the team did what the AGCL asks of every government. They tried to enumerate. If a spending category isn't on the list, the money can't be spent on it. Write down what the money can do, and limit it to that.

The first items were easy. Almost everyone agreed the cost of running the group should be covered. The state filing. Bank fees. The website. Postage for letters to officials. That was the price of existing. You can't trace dollars in the open if the group doesn't exist to hold them.

The second category took longer. Gatherings.

Everybody agreed the team should get together. A team that only meets online forgets it's a team. But should donated money pay for that? The answer they landed on was yes, as long as the gathering built the team. A function with a purpose: welcome new members, close out a project, celebrate reaching CE2.

So they ran a survey. Simple, anonymous, one question. What kind of team-building functions would you support with your donations?

The results came back on a Saturday.

Food-only functions: nearly everyone said yes. A cookout, a potluck, pizza after a work session. No argument.

Then the next line. A beer keg.

The room split almost down the middle.

Half the team thought a keg was the most natural team-building tool ever invented. "You want team building? That's team building."

The other half didn't want their money anywhere near it. Some didn't drink. Some had watched drinking hurt their families. Some just didn't think a civic group should be buying alcohol with donated money, and said so plainly.

Then Ridgeline, the same member who'd asked about the sixty dollars, pointed out the problem nobody had seen coming.

"With the random draw, anybody's blocks could pay for the keg. Mine. Yours. Longbow could type in his code next month and see that his ten dollars bought beer. And he'd have no say in it."

The room went quiet.

The random draw had been designed so nobody could choose whose money paid for what. That was the point. It kept the treasurer honest. It kept donors hidden. But it also meant every dollar in the pool was everyone's dollar. Whatever the team chose to buy, it bought with all of their money at once.

That's when the team understood what they'd really built. Not a bookkeeping system. A small government.

Somebody suggested just putting the keg on the list by majority vote. Somebody else asked whether a majority should be able to spend a minority's money on something the minority opposed. Somebody suggested taking it off the list completely. Somebody else asked whether a majority should be able to stop a team-building function most people wanted.

The meeting ran long. Nobody voted. The admin left the cookout request open, with one comment at the bottom.

"Burgers approved. Keg question tabled to next Saturday."

Longbow drove home thinking about it. The team had spent four months learning how to trace every dollar. It had taken one keg to teach them the harder question.

Not where did the money go.

What is the money allowed to do?

Next Saturday, they'd have to answer it.

Recap. The Sedgwick County team moved from a cash envelope to a registered group running every dollar in the open. The first spending request raised a harder question: what is the money allowed to do? Admin costs and food functions had wide support. A beer keg split the team, because random draws meant anyone's blocks could pay for it.
