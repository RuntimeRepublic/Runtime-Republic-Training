Ninety days went by fast.

At the check meeting, the admin put the three questions back on the screen. Did the function pool get money? Did anyone feel their money was spent on something they opposed? Did the team still feel like a team?

The first answer was yes. The function pool had paid for the keg, a bowling night, and half of a Christmas party.

The third answer was yes too. More people came to meetings now than before the keg fight.

The second answer was the interesting one. Nobody felt their money had been misused. But Mesa, the keg's loudest supporter, had a complaint of her own.

"The function pool works. But it's a bucket. I put twenty dollars in for the keg, and two of my blocks got drawn for bowling. I don't bowl."

The room laughed, but she had a point. The second pool had solved the keg problem and created a smaller version of the same problem inside itself.

The treasurer had been waiting for this. "What if we stop using a pool for functions and use pledges instead?"

He sketched it on the whiteboard. Before any function, an admin posts the plan and the cost. A cookout, forty dollars. Bowling, sixty. Members pledge blocks to that function, and only that function. If the pledges reach the cost, the function happens, and the pledged blocks are drawn. If they don't, the function doesn't happen, and the blocks go back to their owners.

"Not enough blocks, no party," he said.

Mesa liked it immediately. "So I pledge to the keg, and my money can't wander off to bowling."

"Right. And nobody who didn't pledge pays a cent."

Ridgeline, who had never put a dollar toward beer, nodded slowly. "And if the team really wants something, the pledges show it. If they don't, the empty bucket shows that too."

They tested it that month. Four functions were proposed. Three filled their pledges in a week. The fourth, a trip to a minor-league game, sat at half funded for three weeks and quietly expired. No vote. No argument. No hurt feelings. The blocks just went home.

The general pool stayed exactly as it was. Admin costs. Postage. The state filing. The website. The price of existing still came out of everyone's money, because everyone had agreed it should.

For a group, it was close to perfect.

Then Longbow raised his hand.

"This works great for us. Now try it on the county."

Everyone knew what he meant. The team had spent months mapping where their tax dollars went. Property tax. Sales tax. Fees on fees. Now they had a method that let every member decide where their own dollars went, and the obvious next thought was why government couldn't work the same way.

The treasurer shook his head. "It can't. Not like this."

He was right, and Longbow knew it. Government can't run on pledges. Taxes aren't voluntary gifts. Some duties have to be paid for whether a majority pledges or not: courts, defense, the things the Constitution actually lists. A government that waits for enough pledges before it defends the country isn't a government.

"So government keeps taking the money," Ridgeline said. "And we're back where we started."

"Not quite," Longbow said.

He walked to the whiteboard and wrote one sentence.

Government gets one chance to spend it right.

"Taxes come in. Government spends them on enumerated, constitutional purposes. That's the deal. But say government tries to spend your share on something that isn't enumerated, something the Constitution never gave it authority to do. Under taxpayer supremacy, that attempt fails. And your share doesn't go back into government's pool."

"Where does it go?" Mesa asked.

"Into your own sinking fund."

He drew a second box on the board.

"Half of that fund pays down the national debt. The other half stays yours and grows for your retirement. Government doesn't get a second try at that dollar. It's gone, and it's working for you now."

The treasurer frowned, the way he did when he was checking math in his head. "How much is that worth to one person?"

Longbow wrote 72 on the board.

"Rule of 72. Divide 72 by the return, and that's how many years it takes money to double. At six percent, money doubles every twelve years. Say a hundred dollars of yours gets diverted when you're thirty. By sixty-six, that's about eight hundred dollars. Three doublings."

"From one bad spending attempt," Ridgeline said.

"From one. Now think about every non-enumerated program in the county budget, the state budget, the federal budget. Every one of them becomes a number in somebody's account, growing, with their name on it."

The room was quiet.

Mesa said it first. "Government would get real careful real fast."

"That's the point," Longbow said. "Right now, bad spending costs government nothing. The money's already gone. Nobody gets it back. Under this, every unconstitutional dollar is a dollar government loses forever, and a citizen gains forever. The incentive flips."

The admin, who had been quietly writing notes, looked up. "Is this in the AGCL?"

Longbow answered honestly. "Part of it. The Sinking Liberty Fund is there. Paying down the debt is there. Putting non-enumerated programs first in line to go is there. Letting each taxpayer send failed spending into their own account, the Rule of 72 accounts, isn't written down yet. It's a proposal."

"Then let's propose it," the admin said. "With evidence."

That was how the team found its next project. Not a function. A test.

Every member already had their household tax map: every tax they paid, who received it, and whether it passed the control tests. Now they would add one column. For each dollar that failed the enumeration test, how much would be sitting in their sinking fund today, and how much would it grow by retirement?

They'd run it like everything else. Plan, do, check, act. One month of real numbers, from real households, in the open.

Longbow drove home that night thinking about how it had all started. A white envelope in a truck. A sixty-dollar question nobody could answer.

The team had learned to trace its money, then to list what its money was allowed to do. Then it learned to let people choose.

Now it was learning the hardest lesson of all. A group can let people choose. A government has to be made to choose right, once, and pay for it when it doesn't.

And the best way to make that case wasn't a speech.

It was a column of numbers, growing.
