Ninety days went by fast.

At the check meeting, the admin put the three questions back on the screen. The first and third answers were yes. The function pool had paid for the keg, a bowling night, and half of a Christmas party, and more people came to meetings than before.

The second answer was the interesting one. Nobody felt their money had been misused. But Mesa had a complaint of her own.

"The function pool works. But it's a bucket. I put twenty dollars in for the keg, and two of my blocks got drawn for bowling. I don't bowl."

The room laughed, but she had a point. The second pool had solved the keg problem and created a smaller version of it inside itself.

The treasurer had been waiting for this. "Nobody starts building a tower without first sitting down to count the cost. So let's count it before we build."

He sketched it on the whiteboard. Before any function, an admin posts the plan and the cost. A cookout, forty dollars. Bowling, sixty. Members pledge blocks to that function, and only that function. If the pledges reach the cost, the function happens, and the pledged blocks are drawn. If they don't, the function doesn't happen, and the blocks go back to their owners.

"Not enough blocks, no party," he said.

Mesa liked it immediately. "So I pledge to the keg, and my money can't wander off to bowling."

"Right. And nobody who didn't pledge pays a cent."

They tested it that month. Every member's assistant tracked the pledges and posted the totals each night. Four functions were proposed. Three filled their pledges in a week. The fourth, a trip to a minor-league game, sat at half funded for three weeks and quietly expired. No vote. No argument. The blocks just went home.

The general pool stayed as it was. The price of existing still came out of everyone's money, because everyone had agreed it should.

For a group, it was close to perfect.

Then Longbow raised his hand. "This works great for us. Now try it on the county."

The treasurer shook his head. "It can't. Not like this."

He was right. Government can't run on pledges. The duties the Constitution lists, like courts and defense, can't wait for enough pledges.

"So government keeps taking the money," Ridgeline said. "And we're back where we started."

"Not quite," Longbow said. "Hamilton already laid this stone."

He told them the story. In 1790, Alexander Hamilton sent Congress his Report on Public Credit. The new nation was buried in war debt, and Hamilton's answer wasn't to hide it or inflate it away. It was a sinking fund: money set aside and pledged to pay the debt down, so the nation's credit could stand on something solid.

"The AGCL carries that forward. Point 05.1 is named for him. The Alexander Hamilton Power of 72 Sinking Liberty Fund."

He walked to the whiteboard and wrote one sentence.

Government gets one chance to spend it right.

"Under Point 05.1, every federal program is classified, enumerated or non-enumerated. Enumerated is what the Constitution actually lists. Non-enumerated spending can't be paid for with debt, and it expires automatically unless it's reauthorized. If Congress fails to pass a lawful budget, the non-enumerated programs are the first to go."

"And the money that doesn't get spent right?" Mesa asked.

"Surplus reversion. Unspent federal money doesn't stay with the government. It goes back to the citizen's own account."

He drew a second box. "Your Sinking Liberty Fund. Every citizen gets one. Up to a quarter of the federal taxes you pay is credited to it, never less than five percent. It's yours. It's inheritable. Nobody can seize it. And it's on a public blockchain: the government's side is traceable, your side is anonymous."

"And then it just sits there?" the treasurer asked.

"It works. The Treasury has to borrow from citizen funds first, at half the market rate, and the interest is credited to you. Right now, somebody else holds the national debt. Under 05.1, you do."

"How much is that worth to one person?"

Longbow wrote 72 on the board.

"Rule of 72. Divide 72 by the return, and that's how many years money takes to double. At six percent, every twelve years. A hundred dollars in your fund at thirty is about eight hundred by sixty-six. Three doublings."

Mesa said it first. "Government would get real careful real fast."

"That's the point. Right now, bad spending costs government nothing. Under 05.1, non-enumerated spending runs out on its own, and unspent money comes home. The incentive flips."

The admin looked up. "Is all of this in the AGCL?"

"It's Point 05.1. Written down. What isn't written yet is the proof that ordinary households come out ahead. That part's our job."

"Then let's prove it," the admin said.

Every member already had their household tax map. Now each member's assistant would add one column: how much of their federal taxes would have been credited to their own Sinking Liberty Fund, and what it would grow to by retirement. The assistants would run the math. The members would check it. Plan, do, check, act. One month of real numbers, in the open.

Longbow drove home thinking about how it had started. A white envelope in a truck. A sixty-dollar question.

A group can let people choose. A government has to be made to choose right, once, and pay for it when it doesn't.

The best way to make that case wasn't a speech. It was a column of numbers, growing.

Recap. Pledge blocks fixed the function pool: count the cost first, and not enough blocks means no party. Government can't run on pledges, but Hamilton's sinking fund, carried forward in AGCL Point 05.1, gives it one chance to spend right. Non-enumerated spending expires, unspent money returns to each citizen's fund, and the Treasury borrows from citizens first.
