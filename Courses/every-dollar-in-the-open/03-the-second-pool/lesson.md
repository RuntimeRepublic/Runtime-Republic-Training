# The Keg Question, Part 2: The Second Pool

The next Saturday, the whole team showed up. Nothing gets a group to a meeting like beer and money in the same sentence.

The admin put the cookout file on the screen. The burgers had been bought and eaten. Forty-two dollars, five blocks drawn at random, one split with the change returned to the pool. Ridgeline asked his assistant to find his code in the file. One of his blocks was in the cookout. "Good burger," he said. The system was working, and everyone could see it.

Then the admin put up the open question.

Can donated money buy a beer keg for a team-building function?

She didn't ask for a vote. She asked for proposals. Three came in.

The first came from Longbow. Take the keg off the list entirely. If people want beer, they pass a hat at the event, in cash, outside the ledger.

Mesa, one of the keg supporters, pushed back. "So the one thing half of us want goes back to the envelope? We built all this to get out of the envelope. Sweep it out, leave the room empty, and it comes back with friends."

That landed. Nobody wanted cash moving off the books again, even for beer.

The second proposal came from Ridgeline. Keep the keg on the list, but let donors flag their receipt codes. Any code flagged "no alcohol" would be skipped when the draw picked blocks for the keg.

It sounded fair. Then the treasurer asked his assistant to run the numbers on last month's pool.

"Say four of us flag. The keg gets drawn only from the unflagged blocks. The people who didn't flag pay a bigger share of the keg, and the flaggers' blocks get pulled harder for everything else. You're not keeping your money out of the keg. You're pushing the keg onto someone else's money."

Ridgeline thought about it. "So I'm not opting out. I'm just making somebody else pay my share."

Someone wrote it on the board as "good for later, when we're bigger."

The third proposal came from the treasurer. Two pools.

The general pool pays only for what's on the enumerated list: admin costs and food functions. A second pool, the function pool, pays for anything beyond that. A keg. A bowling night. Nobody's money goes into the function pool unless they choose to put it there. When you give, you choose. General, function, or both.

Mesa asked the obvious question. "What if nobody puts money in the function pool?"

"Then there's no keg," the treasurer said. "And that's an answer, too."

Longbow had been reading something on his phone. He looked up.

"Jefferson wrote this in the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom. 'To compel a man to furnish contributions of money for the propagation of opinions which he disbelieves and abhors, is sinful and tyrannical.' He was talking about forcing people to pay for a church they didn't believe in. But the principle is the foundation under everything we're doing. Your money follows your consent."

"So the general pool is what we all consented to," Ridgeline said.

"The cost of existing. And the function pool is what you pay for only if you want it. Nobody pays for the keg unless they choose to. Nobody gets stopped from buying one if enough of them choose to."

Then Longbow said what several people were thinking. "That's the FairTax, too."

He explained it the way he'd explained it a dozen times at the kitchen table. Today, government gets paid first, before the family sees the money. Withholding. Escrow. Liens. Under AGCL Point 01, the family pays tax only when it chooses to buy. The family decides when the money moves.

Mesa grinned. "So beer is a consumption tax."

"Beer has always been a consumption tax."

They didn't make it permanent. The admin wrote it up as a test. Plan, do, check, act. Ninety days. The enumerated list would be adopted. The function pool would open. At the end, they'd check three things.

Did the function pool get money?

Did anyone feel their money was spent on something they opposed?

Did the team still feel like a team?

The vote was the first the team ever took on a rule about its own money. It passed with two no votes, and both of those members stayed. One said, "I don't like it, but I can see exactly what it does. That's more than I can say about my property tax."

The function pool opened the following Monday. By Friday, it had twelve blocks.

Longbow checked his phone that night. His general blocks were in the pool, two of them already spent on postage for letters to county commissioners. He hadn't put anything into the function pool. He didn't plan to.

He scrolled to the function pool's public file. Twelve blocks, twelve scrambled codes, none of them his. Whoever wanted the keg was paying for the keg.

And it didn't bother him at all. Not because he'd changed his mind about beer. Because nobody had taken the choice away from him.

That was the real test. Not whether the team agreed. Whether people who disagreed could stay.

The keg arrived three weeks later, at a work session where the team finished its first county budget map. Mesa tapped it. Ridgeline brought root beer. Longbow brought the charcoal.

Recap. The team weighed three answers to the keg: pass a hat off the books, flag receipt codes, or two pools. It chose two pools as a ninety-day test, built on a principle as old as Jefferson: your money follows your consent. The general pool pays for the enumerated list. The function pool takes only what people choose to give.
