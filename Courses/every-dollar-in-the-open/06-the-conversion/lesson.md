Harvest arrived early. He had a folder under his arm, the first paper folder anyone had brought to a meeting in months.

"My earnings record," he said. "Printed it from my Social Security account online. Every year since 1978."

Longbow had the AGCL open on the screen. Point 04, the Social Security Gold Lockbox. He didn't summarize it. He read it step by step, the way the team read everything, and they tested each step against Harvest's folder.

"Step one. Every month counts on its own."

Under Point 04, every month with actual payroll contributions is evaluated by itself. Both halves count, the part taken from the worker and the part paid by the employer. Medicare contributions count too. Each month's dollars are converted into gold weight using that month's gold price. Then that weight is credited permanently.

"Why gold?" Mesa asked.

"Because a dollar from 1978 isn't a dollar today. Gold weight doesn't shrink when the government prints more money. The AGCL says no averaging across years, and no revaluing at today's prices. What you paid in a month becomes what that month's money could buy in gold. Then it's locked."

Harvest opened his folder. "Here's the problem."

He turned it around so everyone could see. His record listed earnings by year. One number for 1978. One for 1979. One for every year after.

"There are no months on here."

The treasurer leaned in. "So we can't run the conversion the way Point 04 says."

"Not from this," Longbow said. "The rule says month by month. The record we can get is year by year."

The admin wrote it down the way the team had learned to: as evidence, not as a complaint. Point 04 requires monthly contribution data. The public earnings record shows yearly earnings only. Either the monthly records exist inside government and must be released, or the rule needs a defined method for years where only annual data exists.

"Step two," Longbow said. "Only real contributions count."

Point 04 limits the conversion to months with actual payroll payments. No contribution, no credit. And only people who were citizens when they paid qualify. No retroactive adjustments, no political exemptions, no administrative overrides.

"It's not a new benefit," Longbow said. "It's a receipt for what you already paid."

"Step three. Your own ledger."

Every qualifying person gets a Social Security Gold Ledger. It records the ounces you own, the interest you've earned, who's allowed to borrow it, and your beneficiaries.

Harvest asked the question everyone was waiting for. "And my kids?"

"Inheritable. Required beneficiaries. What happened to your brother's forty years doesn't happen under this."

"Step four. First in line."

Point 04 classifies every converted obligation as Priority-One Federal Debt. First to be repaid, ahead of everything else the government owes. No deferral, no dilution, no reclassification. And it's tied into the same Rule of 72 structure as everyone's Sinking Liberty Fund.

"Step five. Nobody touches it without your permission."

Government access is prohibited by default. If government wants to borrow against your ledger, you set the terms: how much, how long, at what interest. Zero is a valid answer.

"Step six. What happens after the payroll tax ends."

Under H.R. 25, the FairTax, payroll taxes go away. Point 04 says future Social Security funding comes from consumption-based revenue instead of wages, and every new dollar is converted to gold weight as it comes in. No new unfunded promises allowed.

"And Medicare?" Harvest asked. "I'm on it now."

Longbow scrolled to Point 10, the American Health Freedom Plan. Medicare becomes a personal account measured in gold. Fully owned. Inheritable. Benefits tied to what you put in. And care for people who are disabled or can't pay is handled through its own funding, so nobody's personal account gets pooled or redistributed to cover it.

Then the admin did what the team always did at the end of a test. She asked for the hard questions, the ones the plan hadn't answered yet.

Ridgeline went first. "Where does the gold come from? That's a lot of gold, and somebody has to guard it."

Point 04 calls it a national-security asset and puts custody on the federal government, fully auditable. It doesn't say how much gold that would take, or where it comes from.

The treasurer went next. "Harvest is collecting right now. Who pays his check while all this gets converted?"

The point doesn't lay out the transition for people already drawing benefits.

Mesa added the last one. "And the people who paid in for years before they became citizens?"

Point 04 excludes them. That would be a hard conversation in a lot of families.

The admin wrote all three on the board under a heading she'd started using for anything the team couldn't answer yet. Open questions. Then she typed them into the team's packet, with the monthly-data finding at the top.

"That's the job," she said. "Not to pretend the plan is finished. To test it with real people, find where it breaks, and say so in public."

Harvest closed his folder.

"You know what's strange," he said. "For forty-six years, money came out of my check, and I never once saw where it went. Tonight, I found out more about my own retirement in two hours than I did in my whole working life."

He looked around the room. A team that had started with a white envelope in a truck. A sixty-dollar question. A fight over a keg.

"You people track ten dollar blocks," he said. "I want somebody tracking mine."

Longbow handed him a receipt code card.

"Start with the small book," he said. "We'll work on the big one together."

Recap. Point 04 converts every month of payroll contributions into gold weight, credited to a personal, inheritable ledger that is first in line for repayment and closed to government without permission. Point 10 does the same for Medicare. The team found one gap, yearly records against a monthly rule, and three open questions.
