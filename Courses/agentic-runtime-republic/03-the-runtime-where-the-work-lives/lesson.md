# The Runtime, Where the Work Lives

In software, the runtime is where a program actually runs. Code on paper is a plan. Code in a runtime is a working system.

The Runtime Republic is what happens when citizens treat self-government the same way. The founding documents are the plan. The runtime is the working system of people, records and checks that carries the plan out every day.

By June of 2027, that runtime has three layers.

The first layer is the repository. It is the workshop. GitHub holds the control documents, bill drafts, letters, scorecards and decisions. Every change is recorded. Every version can be compared to the last. If someone asks why the team chose a certain approach in February, the answer is there in writing, with a name and a date beside it.

The second layer is the agent. It is the Guide. Each volunteer has a Claude Project connected to the team's repository. That's where the machinist went first on Tuesday morning, and that's where most of the work now starts. The Guide reads everything in the repository. It drafts letters, checks math, cites statutes and explains bills in plain English. It can tell a new volunteer in five minutes what took the founding members five months to learn. But it never decides. Every draft waits for a human to approve it.

The third layer is social media. It is the front porch. X, Facebook, Discord and YouTube are where people meet the work, ask questions and get recruited. But nothing important is stored on the porch. The posts point back to the repository, because a Facebook thread can disappear and a repository can't be quietly rewritten.

And sometimes the front porch is a real front porch.

Members of the Sedgwick County chapter order door hangers online, a few dozen at a time. Each one carries five QR codes. One opens the county property-tax tracker, so a neighbor can see how many homes in their own county are headed to tax sale. One opens the chapter's Facebook group. One opens the Discord, where the volunteers do the actual work. One plays a three-minute Exit Point video that explains the problem in plain English. And one opens the form to order a yard sign.

Members walk their own blocks on a Saturday morning and hang them on doorknobs. They hand them out after church, at the hardware store, at the ball field, anywhere they end up talking with a fellow property taxpayer. The conversation might last two minutes. The door hanger keeps working long after it ends.

The Guide helps here too. When a member scans their stack of hangers before heading out, the Project logs which streets they covered, so two neighbors don't walk the same block twice. A week later, the team can see which neighborhoods are joining the Facebook group and which haven't been reached yet.

That's the path in. A neighbor finds a door hanger, scans a code, watches a video, joins the Discord, and a month later is approving posts from their own Claude Project. Paper on a doorknob leads to a seat at the workbench.

Each layer does what it's good at. The repository remembers. The Guide does the heavy lifting. The porch, online and off, brings people in.

The rule that holds it all together fits on one line:

AI drafts. Humans decide. The repository remembers.

Break that rule, and you get either a machine running people or people drowning in work. Keep it, and ordinary citizens can do in an evening what used to take a paid staff a week.
