This is The Agentic Runtime Republic, a futuristic essay by James Hodges. It is a look forward to June 1, 2027. The scenes are imagined. The repositories, control documents and methods named here are real and are being built today.

It is Tuesday, June 1, 2027, in Sedgwick County, Kansas. Nobody is driving to a meeting.

The legislative session in Topeka ended a few weeks ago. In past years, that meant citizens went home and waited until January. This year is different.

A retired machinist opens his phone and sees a Discord ping from his county property-tax team. He doesn't scroll through a long chat thread to figure out what happened. He opens his Claude Project and works from there.

The Project is connected to the team's GitHub repository, so it already knows the plan, the history, and his role. He asks what changed overnight. It tells him in plain English. The weekly check ran. The foreclosure tracker has a new count of county homes headed to tax sale. A draft post for the coalition's Facebook Page is waiting for human approval.

He reads the draft right there in the Project and asks Claude to check one number against the tracker. It matches. He fixes one sentence and approves the post before his coffee is cold. The change is recorded in the repository with his name, the date, and the reason. Nobody has to ask later who approved it.

Down the road, a second county's coalition chapter is going live this week. They didn't build their plan from scratch. They copied the Sedgwick County pilot from GitHub, changed the county name, and inherited every lesson the first chapter learned this spring.

In Topeka, a state representative's office gets a pull request. It is not a protest. It is a proposed draft for next session, with the statute cited, the fiscal impact shown, and a sunset date written in. The aide has never seen a constituent send something this finished. She reads the AI summary, sees one question flagged, and replies asking for a call.

In Washington, the 120th Congress is five months old. The FairTax is back as H.R. 25, as it has been in Congress after Congress. Liberty-Ledger's weekly check shows one new Kansas cosponsor on H.R. 25. The contact log shows three team members wrote to that office in March. Each letter was respectful and factual, and made one clear ask: cosponsor the FairTax. The record says "cosponsored after contact." Not because of contact. After. Honest records are what make the work trustworthy.

By evening, a three-minute Exit Point video explains the week in plain English: what H.R. 25 would do, who signed on, and what comes next. It goes to YouTube, X and the county groups. Every claim in the video links back to a line in a repository anyone can read.

No one in this story is a professional lobbyist. No one waited for permission. The Runtime Republic hasn't arrived all at once. It is running quietly, one county and one commit at a time.
