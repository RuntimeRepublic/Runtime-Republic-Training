The second group to arrive is legislators. The first ones to arrive gain a real edge.

By June of 2027, a few Kansas lawmakers have noticed something. The constituents who reach them through the runtime don't show up angry and empty-handed. They show up with finished work.

A lawmaker who works in the open can do things a closed office can't.

They can publish drafts early. When a bill sits in a public repository, constituents can read it, question it and improve it before it's introduced, not after it's already locked in committee.

They can accept proposals as pull requests. Instead of a stack of angry emails, the office gets specific edits with citations. Staff can accept them, reject them or comment on them, and every decision stays on the record.

They can show the fiscal math. The Guide can build a first-pass fiscal note in minutes. Staff verify it. The public can check it. Nobody has to take anyone's word for what a bill will cost.

They can build sunsets in by default. If a program has no end date, the template catches it before it ever reaches committee. That's AGCL Control 00A, doing its job automatically.

And they can attach a real test. Every draft carries its own Plan, Do, Check, Act plan, with review dates set before the bill passes and results measured in the lives of real households. A lawmaker who signs a bill like that is making a promise that can be checked.

This is the work the Legislative-Manager and Constitutional-Engineering repositories were built to do. They engineer Kansas bills the way aerospace engineers build parts. Every authority is cited. Every unknown is named. Nothing moves forward by implication. If a piece is missing, the package stops until a human fixes it.

On the other side of the desk, Liberty-Ledger keeps score. It tracks how every official handles bills within the AGCL's scope, from H.R. 25 in Congress to property-tax bills in Topeka. It logs each team contact with an office and follows it through Plan, Do, Check, Act to an outcome.

The scoring method is published, and it applies to every party the same way. A Republican who ignores property owners gets the same mark as a Democrat who does. An official the team likes gets measured by the same rule as one the team doesn't. Without that, a scorecard is just a weapon. With it, it's a record people can trust.

And the record stays honest. Liberty-Ledger writes "cosponsored after contact," never "because of contact." The team doesn't claim credit it can't prove.

Some legislators will stay away from all this. They'll find the openness uncomfortable, and that's their right. But the ones who step into the runtime will find something they've never had before: thousands of constituents who read the bill, checked the math, and sent a clean edit with the statute cited.

That's not pressure. That's help. And in a legislature where staff are stretched thin, help is the most persuasive thing a citizen can offer.
