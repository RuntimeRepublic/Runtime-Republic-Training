# The Honest Risks and the Real Test

The AGCL's own Control 00F says no system may operate without declaring how it can fail. A movement built on that standard has to hold itself to it too. So here are the risks, stated plainly.

The first risk is that AI makes mistakes. A Guide can misread a statute, get a number wrong, or invent a citation that sounds real but isn't. That's not a rare event. It happens. The defense is the rule that never bends. Humans approve every draft. Every claim links to a source. Anything that hasn't been confirmed is labeled unverified.

The second risk is that platforms can change the rules. X, Facebook, Discord, GitHub, even the AI companies are all private businesses. Any one of them can change its terms or shut down an account. That's why the work lives in repositories that can be copied and moved, and why social media is the front porch, not the house.

The third risk is that openness can be used against you. That's why personal information stays out of public files, always. No home addresses. No phone numbers. The work is public. The people doing it are protected.

The fourth risk is that scorecards can turn into score-settling. The method has to be public, and it has to be applied the same way to every official in every party. No exceptions for friends.

The fifth risk is that speed can outrun judgment. A bill written in an afternoon can carry a mistake that hurts people for a decade.

And there's one more risk, the biggest of all. Tools can make people feel like they're doing more than they are. A thousand approved posts don't equal one changed vote. Even a passed bill isn't proof of anything. A law can pass with good intentions and still fail the people it was meant to help.

That's why the Runtime Republic adds a real test.

Starting now, every bill the movement writes or supports must carry its own Plan, Do, Check, Act plan, written right into the bill package. Not as a promise. As a requirement.

The Plan states what the bill is supposed to fix and how we'll know. For a property-tax bill, that might be fewer homes lost to tax sale, lower bills for fixed-income owners, or a shorter appeals process. Each goal gets a number and a starting point measured before the law takes effect.

The Do is the law itself, with its effective date and its sunset date.

The Check is a scheduled review, set on the calendar before the bill ever passes. Six months after it takes effect. One year. Two years. At each review, the results are measured against the starting point, using real data from real households. Not projections. Not talking points. Lived results.

The Act is what happens next. If the law worked, it continues, and the lessons are written into the next bill's template. If it partly worked, it gets amended. If it failed, it ends, the way AGCL Control 00A says every authority must when it no longer solves the problem it was created for.

The foreclosure tracker becomes part of that test. So does Liberty-Ledger. The same weekly checks that track bills and contacts now track outcomes. Did the homes stay with their owners? Did the bills go down? Did the people the law promised to help actually get helped?

This changes the conversation with legislators, too. A lawmaker who sponsors a bill with a built-in test is making a promise they can be measured against. That's a lot harder to ignore than a press release. And it's fair. It applies to our own bills just as strictly as anyone else's.

The measure that matters is the real world: homes kept, burdens lifted, trust earned. If a law can't show that, it doesn't deserve to stay on the books, no matter who wrote it.

None of these risks is a reason to stop. They're reasons to be careful. A movement that names its own weak points, and tests its own work, is much harder to break than one that pretends it never makes mistakes.
